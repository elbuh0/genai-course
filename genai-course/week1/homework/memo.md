Architecture Decision Memo: VAE Recommended for Synthetic Data Generation

We recommend a Variational Auto Encoder (VAE) architecture to synthetically augment a training dataset for a diabetic retinopathy classifier primarily due to the project constraint of a limited dataset consisting of 800 real images across five severity levels. VAEs may also help model intermediary disease progression between severity levels via interpolation. 

The primary failure mode of a VAE architecture will be unusably blurry or nonsensical image outputs. While a simple visual inspection of an output sample grid should reveal this failure mode prior to production, plausible, seemingly useable images may fail silently by omitting fine-grained pathological features required by the safety-critical downstream classifier to effectively detect disease. Accordingly, in addition to an initial output visual inspection, we recommend a waterfall methodology of automated (see below) and human SME checks to ensure VAE synthetic data reliably reproduce clinically informative features. 

We rejected GAN and Diffusion architectures—despite their potential to produce higher-quality images useful to the downstream classifier—as both are far too “data hungry” for the 800-image constraint of this project, requiring in the case of the former architecture at least 10K or more images. A GAN architecture would likely enter varietal mode collapse with an 800-image dataset, as its generator would likely fail to learn the data’s distribution with so few training examples. While a Diffusion architecture would not fail in such a manner, overfitting would likely occur wherein the model learned to produce high-quality images that closely matched limited training data but failed to represent the real-world universe of images recording the disease and its progression—crucial for the safety-critical downstream classifier.

We propose the use of quantitative evaluation metrics beyond FID such as Reconstruction Error Masking or Anomaly Scoring; these metrics respectively flag local pixel-level errors beyond a certain threshold or VAE generated outputs excessively far from the known training distributions. Aside from failing to detect varietal mode collapse (admittedly less an issue given we are opting for a VAE vs. GAN architecture), FID alone is insufficient as it is a global statistic that may not necessarily penalize an image for failing to represent less common or local yet clinically relevant features that do not survive a VAE’s compression and latent-space regularization towards a normal distribution. We also advise simply testing the downstream classifier’s performance prior to production on synthetic data scored in such a manner (possibly alongside a limited human review, as suggested above)—if the classifier’s performance improves, then the VAE had effectively learned clinically relevant features.

Finally, a VAE may also help model disease progression between severity levels  by enabling the exploration of the latent space between known samples. While not an objective of this project, it is a possible secondary benefit.
